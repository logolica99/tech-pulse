Nvidia has told the companies that build servers around its chips to stop shipping them to buyers in Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia, according to a Financial Times report citing three people with knowledge of the matter. Partners including Dell and Supermicro have told customers in those four countries that Nvidia won't let their orders go out.

## Why now

The halt follows the US Commerce Department rule that took effect last week, which requires export licenses for advanced chips going to several Southeast Asian countries. Washington's aim is to close the route Chinese AI developers have used to reach top Nvidia hardware: renting it from cloud providers in the region instead of importing chips into China. We covered that rule when it was announced: [/posts/2026-11-17-us-rule-remote-access-ai-chips-overseas-data-centers](/posts/2026-11-17-us-rule-remote-access-ai-chips-overseas-data-centers).

The people the FT spoke to said it isn't clear whether the pause is temporary while Nvidia and its partners figure out how to comply.

## What's at stake

- Nvidia booked US$104 billion of revenue through its Singapore subsidiary in 2025, up 72% in a year, according to company accounts cited by the FT. That unit also books some Taiwan and China revenue.
- Cloud providers have 90 days to get a license by showing their end users don't include restricted entities. One cloud executive told the FT there is "no guidance on execution" and called the situation "sheer chaos."
- Singapore-based Aolani, which planned to lease computing power to ByteDance, is among those affected; the FT says some banks stopped issuing roughly $500 million in credit for that project.

Nvidia declined to comment to the FT, and Dell, Supermicro, ByteDance and Aolani did not respond.
