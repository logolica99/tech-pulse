Seattle startup Zeno Power says it has completed a two-week demonstration of its maritime nuclear battery hardware in open water off Washington state. The prototype, a Stirling generator, was lowered to the seabed, produced more than 20 watts of electricity and was then recovered. The company says it is the first time a generator of this kind has made power on the seabed.

## No radioactive fuel yet

The test did not involve any nuclear material. An electric heating element stood in for the heat source so the team could check the generator, the materials and the deployment and recovery process at sea. In the finished product, heat will come from the decay of strontium-90, a radioisotope that can be recovered from spent nuclear fuel, and the Stirling engine will turn that heat into electricity by cycling gas between hot and cold phases.

Zeno chief product officer Lindsey Boles told GeekWire the company expects its design to be at least four times more efficient than the traditional radioisotope thermoelectric generators used on spacecraft such as Voyager. That is a company claim, not a measured result for a fueled unit.

## What comes next

The work is backed by the Department of Defense, following an initial $7.5 million contract in 2023. The company says the prototype went from final design to open-water testing in under 16 months.

Next year, Zeno plans to fit a radioisotope heat source into the generator and test it in a controlled water tank. If that goes well, it aims for its first operational seabed deployment of a fueled system later in 2027. The target uses are long-lived power for undersea sensor networks and recharging stations for autonomous underwater vehicles, places where ordinary batteries run down fast and solar power is not an option.
