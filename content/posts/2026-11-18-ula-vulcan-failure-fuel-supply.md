United Launch Alliance has narrowed down why its Vulcan Centaur rocket failed on Sunday, Nov. 15, a launch that lost 40 Amazon Leo internet satellites.

## What ULA found

In an update on Wednesday, ULA said preliminary indications are that the first-stage booster's low thrust came from inadequate fuel supply from the vehicle to its two BE-4 engines, not from how the engines themselves performed. The company stressed that work remains to determine the root cause.

ULA said the booster underperformed shortly after liftoff. By the time the stages separated, the rocket was too low and too slow to reach orbit, and telemetry was lost about 10 minutes into the flight. The six Northrop Grumman solid rocket boosters, which were redesigned after a nozzle problem on Vulcan's February flight, and the Centaur V upper stage with its Aerojet Rocketdyne RL10 engines all appear to have worked as planned.

## Why it matters beyond Vulcan

The finding is good news for Blue Origin, which builds the BE-4. The same engine powers the first stage of its New Glenn rocket, which has been grounded since a test-stand explosion in late May that Blue Origin traced to a main oxygen valve. Blue Origin CEO Dave Limp said he was confident in ULA's team and looked forward to Vulcan's return to flight.

## What's next

The FAA is overseeing ULA's mishap investigation, and it is not yet clear how long Vulcan will stay grounded. Sunday's flight was Vulcan's fifth, its first complete failure, and the first of 38 Vulcan launches planned for Amazon Leo. Amazon's next batch of satellites is set to fly on an Arianespace Ariane 6, targeted for Nov. 23.
