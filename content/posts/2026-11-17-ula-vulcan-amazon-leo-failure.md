United Launch Alliance's Vulcan rocket lifted off from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station on Sunday, November 15, carrying 40 satellites for Amazon's Leo broadband network. Roughly ten minutes into the flight, the vehicle suffered what ULA called an anomaly and failed to reach orbit. All 40 satellites were lost.

## What we know

ULA, the Boeing and Lockheed Martin joint venture, said it did not yet have enough data to explain what happened and would work with Amazon and others to find the cause and return to flight. CNBC reports the failure appeared to occur after the upper stage separated from the BE-4-powered first stage, and that Blue Origin, which builds the BE-4, is involved in the investigation. It is ULA's first mission failure since the company was formed in 2006.

The mission, LV-01, was the first of 38 Vulcan launches Amazon has bought. It was also Vulcan's first flight in nine months, after investigations into its solid rocket boosters and engines, and the first Vulcan to fly with six solid boosters. CNBC notes the Pentagon had been waiting on this flight before clearing Vulcan to resume national security missions.

## Impact on Amazon Leo

Amazon has roughly 400 Leo satellites in orbit, about 12% of the 3,236 needed for global coverage, according to CNBC. Amazon told Light Reading the loss does not change its plan to begin commercial service in 2026, saying it has enough satellites for continuous coverage at some latitudes and hundreds more ready to fly. Its next launch, on an Arianespace rocket, is planned for no earlier than November 23.

## The bigger picture

A lengthy investigation could keep Vulcan grounded for months. That deepens a US launch bottleneck at a time when SpaceX has stopped taking new Falcon 9 bookings, CNBC reports. Analysts quoted by Light Reading say the failure further strengthens SpaceX's dominance. The same weekend, SpaceX caught a Starship upper stage at its launch tower for the first time.
