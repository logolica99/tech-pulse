Levent Alpöge, a mathematician who works at Anthropic, says he has proved the Riemann hypothesis with help from Claude. The 1859 conjecture about how prime numbers are distributed is one of the seven Millennium Prize problems and among the most famous open questions in mathematics.

Alpöge posted the claim on his own website, and suggests he held it back until after Anthropic's IPO on Thursday. He says the method also settles the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and that he and Claude formalized the argument in Lean, a language that lets a computer check every step of a proof. In his essay he describes messaging Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei about it the previous Sunday night. The Verge reports that Anthropic itself has not commented or taken part in the announcement.

## Where verification stands

The proof has not been formally accepted. The Clay Mathematics Institute, which offers $1 million for each Millennium problem, requires a published solution to hold up for at least two years and win general acceptance before it can be considered.

Early signs are notable, though. TechCrunch reports that several mathematicians, including MIT's Andrew Sutherland, say they have compiled Alpöge's Lean proof successfully, and that experts discussing it online appear fairly confident the formal result holds. What is still missing is a human-readable explanation; Alpöge has said he intends to work on one.

## A human proof, too

On the same day, Stanford professor Ravi Vakil posted a separate, entirely human proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis by Stanford postdoc Alessandro Masullo, the result of 12 years of work. That one is not written in Lean, so it is too early to say whether it holds.
