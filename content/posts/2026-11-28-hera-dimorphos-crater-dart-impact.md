The European Space Agency's Hera spacecraft has sent back its first close look at Dimorphos, the small asteroid that NASA's DART spacecraft crashed into on purpose in September 2022. ESA released the images on November 25 at the Museum für Naturkunde in Berlin.

## What Hera found

DART shortened Dimorphos's orbit around its larger partner, Didymos, by 33 minutes, far more than predicted. Telescopes measured that change, but nobody knew what the impact did to the asteroid. Hera's answer:

- **A huge crater.** The side DART hit has been flattened into a crater the size of a sports stadium that spans the whole width of the 151-metre moonlet.
- **The rest looks the same.** Boulder fields on other parts of Dimorphos match DART's 2022 images.
- **It is tumbling.** As more images came in, the team saw that Dimorphos is now tumbling chaotically in its orbit.
- **Hundreds of boulders.** Long exposures show hundreds of previously unknown boulders, a few metres across, orbiting the pair. The team will have to plan around them as Hera moves closer.

Hera principal investigator Patrick Michel said scientists had debated whether Dimorphos got a crater, changed shape or stayed the same, and that the answer was "all three at once."

## What's next

Hera is flying about 25 to 30 km from the asteroids, using three instruments: a framing camera, the multi-spectral Hyperscout H and JAXA's thermal imager TIRI. In December it should move to about 13 km, and in January 2027 it plans to release two shoebox-sized CubeSats, Juventas and Milani, to get closer still. ESA's next planetary defence mission, Ramses, will study asteroid Apophis during its close Earth flyby in April 2029.
