NASA says two of its Mars missions are dealing with separate hardware problems that have put science on hold.

## Perseverance: both relay radios sidelined

On Oct. 30, Perseverance's primary UHF radio, which sends large amounts of data to orbiters for relay to Earth, stopped sending its regular "heartbeat" signals to the rover's main computer. After 25 missed heartbeats, about 100 seconds of silence, onboard fault protection switched off the radio, moved to the backup and put the rover into safe mode.

Once the rover was recovered, engineers found that the backup UHF radio was transmitting at only about half its expected power. The team has stopped using both radios until it understands what went wrong. For now, Perseverance talks directly to NASA's Deep Space Network over X-band through its high-gain antenna. That link is much slower, so science and instrument work, including new camera images, are paused to save bandwidth for diagnostics.

NASA says the rover is stable and in contact, and the team hopes it can return to science by the end of November.

## Odyssey: a second wheel fails

Separately, the 2001 Mars Odyssey orbiter went into safe mode between Nov. 4 and 6. Engineers found that one of its reaction wheels, which turn the spacecraft without using fuel, had stopped spinning. Odyssey has four wheels, one of which already failed in 2013, and it needs at least three to hold its orientation.

Without that third wheel, Odyssey has to point itself with hydrazine thrusters, and NASA estimates it has only enough fuel to do that for one to two months. In one test the wheel moved when commanded, and more tests are under way to see if it can be recovered. NASA hopes to bring Odyssey back to science and relay work during the last week of November.

Odyssey reached Mars in October 2001 on a mission planned for about 2.5 years. It has operated there longer than any other spacecraft and is part of NASA's Mars relay network for surface missions.
