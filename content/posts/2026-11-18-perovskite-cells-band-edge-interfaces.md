Perovskite solar cells are closing in on silicon, but much of their remaining efficiency loss happens at one spot: the boundary where the light-absorbing perovskite hands electrons to the electron-transport layer. A paper published in Nature on Nov. 18 tackles that boundary directly.

## The problem with today's fixes

Researchers usually coat that interface with modifier molecules to reduce defects. According to the paper, many common ammonium-based modifiers bond only weakly, electronically speaking, with the perovskite surface, and can end up getting in the way of electrons trying to leave.

## A better-connected interface

The team designed a family of fully conjugated formamidinium-based molecules containing heteroatoms. These link electronically with the perovskite surface and extend its band edge into the interface, so electrons can move across more freely while losses from non-radiative recombination drop.

## The results

- Inverted single-junction perovskite cells reached a certified power-conversion efficiency of 27.96%, with a stabilized output of 27.84%.
- The cells kept more than 95% of their starting efficiency after 2,000 hours of continuous operation under one-sun light.
- Applying the same approach to monolithic perovskite-silicon tandem cells gave a certified stabilized output of 34.71%.

The work involved groups including Xi'an Jiaotong University, Fudan University, EPFL (where Michael Grätzel is among the corresponding authors) and the National University of Singapore. The authors argue the approach avoids the usual trade-off between passivating defects and extracting charge. These are lab-scale research cells, and the paper does not address commercial production.
