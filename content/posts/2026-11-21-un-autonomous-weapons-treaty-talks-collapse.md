A week-long push to start negotiating a legally binding treaty on autonomous weapons fell apart in Geneva early on Saturday, after all-night talks could not get past Russian objections, The Guardian reports.

## What happened

The talks took place at the UN's Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW), which works by consensus: all 128 parties have to agree. Most states wanted a mandate that would lead to a binding treaty, but because one objection is enough to block it, the conference adopted a far weaker mandate asking countries to "elaborate by consensus" an instrument that may carry no legal obligations at all.

Diplomats described Russia as deliberately stalling. But it was not alone in resisting a binding law. According to the report, the US said human control over autonomous weapons had come up in meetings between Donald Trump and Xi Jinping at the APEC summit this week, yet it was reluctant to commit to a treaty. South Korea, Japan, Turkey, the UK, France and Germany also favoured a nonbinding agreement.

## Who wanted a treaty

More than 100 states had backed a new law. A group of seven countries (Austria, Belgium, Brazil, Ireland, Mexico, New Zealand and Panama) pushed for a treaty to be negotiated within a year. Human Rights Watch's Bonnie Docherty argued treaties can be negotiated in about 15 months and dismissed a US proposal of five years just to settle a mandate. Max Tegmark of the Future of Life Institute said states had agreed to keep talking about the issue for a 14th year.

## Why it matters

The CCW has not passed a new weapons law in about 30 years, which is why past bans, such as the 2008 cluster munitions treaty, were negotiated outside it. Supporters of a binding autonomous-weapons treaty may now look for a similar route.
